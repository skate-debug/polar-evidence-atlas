# Arctic Sea-Ice Microbiome Research: Literature Assessment and Opportunities for Diver-Assisted Sampling

**Assessment date:** August 18, 2026  
**Scope:** Microorganisms in marine Arctic sea ice, especially ice collected by divers and retained on filter membranes for diversity and metagenomic analysis

## Executive answer

**Yes—Arctic sea-ice microbiomes have already been studied.** Published work spans microscopy and cultivation, 16S and 18S rRNA surveys, shotgun and genome-resolved metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, viromics, long-read sequencing, and, most recently, community methylomics. The field has established that sea ice contains active and compositionally distinctive communities of bacteria, archaea, microalgae, heterotrophic and parasitic protists, fungi, and viruses. These communities vary strongly with ice depth, age, structure, season, snow cover, light, salinity, nutrients, algal biomass, and connection to seawater.

The proposed work is therefore **not novel simply because it will sequence microbes from Arctic ice**. Its potential novelty lies elsewhere. Divers have previously collected bottom-ice algae and under-ice aggregates, but a standardized, replicated, diver-assisted survey that directly samples the underside biofilm and combines prokaryotic, eukaryotic, functional, and viral analyses is not yet a routine or well-developed approach in the literature reviewed here. A strong project would compare diver-collected undersurface material with matched bottom-core sections, upper and interior ice, and under-ice water while preserving exact spatial and environmental information.

The scientific opportunity is substantial, but a filter membrane alone is not an interpretable sample. The study must document what was collected, the sampled area or original ice volume, ice horizon, ice type, melt treatment, fraction retained, filtered volume, controls, storage, and accompanying physical and chemical measurements.

### Direct answers to the original questions

| Question | Evidence-based answer |
|---|---|
| Has anyone studied the Arctic sea-ice microbiome? | Yes. Direct studies extend from vertical bacterial surveys in the 1990s to multi-domain and genome-resolved studies in 2025–2026. |
| Has microbial diversity been studied? | Yes. Bacterial, archaeal, protistan, algal, and fungal diversity have been examined by microscopy, cultivation, FISH, 16S/18S/ITS markers, and metagenomics. |
| Have sea-ice metagenomes been studied? | Yes. Shotgun studies have compared ice with seawater, reconstructed MAGs, and identified carbon, sulfur, and nitrogen metabolic potential. |
| Have Arctic sea-ice viruses been studied? | Yes, although less completely. Sea-ice and cryopeg viromes, cellular-fraction viral sequences, host associations, and viral methylation have been reported. RNA viruses and free-virus fractions remain under-sampled. |

| Can DNA metagenomics establish ecological function? | It establishes functional **potential**. Demonstrating activity or process rates requires RNA, proteins, metabolites, stable-isotope or substrate-incubation measurements, or direct biogeochemical rate assays. |

## 1. Scope and terminology

This assessment interprets “Arctic ice” as **marine sea ice**. Glacier ice, Greenland Ice Sheet ice, terrestrial lake ice, permafrost, and cryopegs are distinct systems with different contamination controls and ecological interpretations. Cryopeg studies are included only where they directly compare ancient hypersaline brine with nearby sea-ice brine.

A diver may collect several biologically different materials:

- A solid piece of bottom ice, including the porous skeletal layer.
- Material scraped or suctioned from the ice underside.
- A floating or attached ice-algal aggregate.
- Slush, frazil, or “rotten” ice.
- A mixture of bottom-ice material and under-ice seawater.

These cannot all be labeled simply as “ice microbiome.” A sample removed from the underside by scraping or suction is most accurately described as an **undersurface biofilm or bottom-ice–water-interface sample**. A solid piece with a known thickness is a **bottom-ice section**. A filter made from a seawater-rich slurry represents an operational mixture unless its solid and liquid components are quantified.

The literature review emphasizes peer-reviewed primary studies published through August 18, 2026. It is a focused evidence assessment rather than a formal systematic review; it prioritizes studies that directly analyzed Arctic marine sea ice, its brine, its underside, or immediately connected habitats.

## 2. What research has already been conducted?

### 2.1 Development of the field

Early Arctic studies established that bacteria were vertically structured and metabolically active within ice rather than merely preserved there. [Gradinger and Zhang (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s003000050139) documented vertical distributions in Barents and Laptev Sea ice. [Brinkmeyer et al. (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.69.11.6610-6619.2003) combined cultivation, 16S rRNA clone libraries, and FISH to compare Arctic and Antarctic pack-ice bacteria. [Junge et al. (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.70.1.550-557.2004) measured bacterial activity at temperatures down to −20°C. [Collins et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1462-2920.2010.02179.x) showed that bacterial and archaeal assemblages persisted through the Arctic winter.

Marker-gene sequencing then revealed taxonomic differentiation among ice habitats. [Eddie et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1462-2920.2009.02126.x) found vertically differentiated eukaryotic communities in landfast sea ice. [Bachy et al. (2011)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2011.00106) recovered diverse microbial eukaryotes from snow, sea ice, and seawater near the North Pole at the end of polar night. [Bowman et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2011.76) demonstrated that multiyear ice and surface seawater hosted distinct 16S-defined communities. [Piwosz et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2013.39) identified diverse picoeukaryotic trophic strategies in Canadian first-year ice.

Subsequent work moved toward ecological succession and function. [Eronen-Rasimus et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1758-2229.12428) resolved vertical and horizontal bacterial variation across a winter-to-spring transition. [Fernández-Méndez et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2016.01884) examined diazotroph diversity across sea ice, melt ponds, and surface waters. [Yergeau et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep42242) combined 16S profiling at 23 Canadian Arctic locations with shotgun metagenomics on selected ice and seawater samples. [Rapp et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01035) traced bacterial connectivity among ice, ice-algal aggregates, water, and the seafloor.

Modern studies now resolve genomes, viruses, metabolic pathways, and regulation. [Zhong et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1128/msystems.00246-20) produced sea-ice and cryopeg viral metagenomes. [Rapp et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2021.701186) compared genomic adaptation and gene expression in sea-ice and cryopeg brine microbiomes. [Thiele et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms10081618) followed bacterial and archaeal succession from winter to summer. [Bellas et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43705-023-00337-2) reconstructed MAGs associated with a bottom-ice diatom bloom and identified nitrogen- and sulfur-related metabolic potential.

The most recent work substantially expands the baseline. The MOSAiC catalogue contains 2,407 prokaryotic and 56 eukaryotic MAGs recovered from 73 samples spanning water, under-ice water, melt ponds, ridges, and sea ice ([Boulton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-025-04525-8)). [Venkatachalam et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.01291-25) reported vertical taxonomic structure and genome-resolved carbon and sulfur metabolism in different Central Arctic ice types. [Kanaan and Deming (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/ismejo/wraf198) used nanopore sequencing to provide the first community-scale analysis of bacterial and viral DNA methylation across sea-ice horizons. [Müller et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-026-03364-8) showed that pressure ridges contain structurally complex, biomass-rich, and compositionally distinctive microbial habitats. [Frantz et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms14020482) characterized rotten sea ice as a distinct microbial habitat, [Cardozo-Mino et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsr2.2026.105607) traced microbial and molecular export during sea-ice melt, and [Nõlvak et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsmc/xtag022) examined microbial succession and hydrocarbon-degradation potential following experimental oil and dispersant exposure.

## 3. What has been learned?

### 3.1 Sea ice is a living, selective habitat

Sea ice is not sterile frozen seawater. Microorganisms occupy liquid brine channels, pockets, surfaces, particles, and biofilms. Some bacteria remain active at very low temperatures, and communities can persist through polar night ([Junge et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.70.1.550-557.2004); [Collins et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1462-2920.2010.02179.x)). Freezing concentrates salts and dissolved and particulate organic matter, while extracellular polymeric substances help organisms retain water, attach to surfaces, and tolerate osmotic stress.

Ice and adjacent seawater exchange organisms, but the ice community is not simply a concentrated seawater inoculum. Studies repeatedly find distinct taxonomic and functional profiles in ice, brine, snow, melt ponds, water, aggregates, and sediments ([Bowman et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2011.76); [Yergeau et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep42242); [Maccario et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-38744-y)). This indicates strong environmental selection after incorporation or immigration.

### 3.2 Vertical position is a major ecological variable

The top, interior, and bottom of one core can represent different ecosystems. Upper ice experiences stronger temperature, brine-salinity, irradiance, and permeability extremes. Bottom ice is connected to seawater, is generally less saline at the microscale during growth, receives nutrients from below, and often supports algae-rich biofilms. Vertical differences have been documented for bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes ([Gradinger & Zhang, 1997](https://doi.org/10.1007/s003000050139); [Eddie et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1462-2920.2009.02126.x); [Eronen-Rasimus et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/1758-2229.12428); [Torstensson et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3354/meps14213); [Kanaan & Deming, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/ismejo/wraf198)).

Consequently, an underside sample should not be generalized to “the whole ice microbiome.” It may, however, be the most biologically productive and ecologically consequential part of the ice and therefore a strong target in its own right.

### 3.3 Season and ice history drive succession

Winter communities persist with low light and often rely on heterotrophy or chemolithotrophy. Ammonia-oxidizing archaea, including *Candidatus Nitrosopumilus*-related organisms, can be important in winter. Increasing spring light promotes diatom and other algal production; algal exudates then select for organisms capable of degrading proteins and complex carbohydrates, including recurrent members of Bacteroidia and Gammaproteobacteria. Bloom decline, melt, and flushing reorganize the community and transfer cells and organic matter to under-ice water and deeper habitats ([Eronen-Rasimus et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/1758-2229.12428); [Thiele et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms10081618); [Dilliplaine et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/1462-2920.70211); [Cardozo-Mino et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsr2.2026.105607)).

First-year, second-year, and multiyear ice can preserve different formation histories, temperatures, salinities, nutrient regimes, and source communities. [Venkatachalam et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.01291-25) found microbial differences among ice types and vertical horizons. Historical microscopy records also suggest that loss of multiyear ice has coincided with declining Central Arctic protist diversity ([Hop et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2020.00243)).

### 3.4 Physical habitat structure matters beyond a simple level-ice core

Level first-year ice is not a sufficient model for all Arctic sea ice. Ridges, refrozen leads, melt ponds, snow–ice interfaces, porous rotten ice, and aggregates create different microhabitats. [Fernández-Méndez et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2018.00075) identified algal hotspots at ridges and the snow–ice interface. [Müller et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-026-03364-8) showed that ridge voids and surrounding ice supported unique microbial communities and high biomass. [Frantz et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms14020482) demonstrated that rotten ice is also microbiologically distinct.

Divers are especially valuable here because they can see and target underside heterogeneity that a blind top-down core can miss: discoloration, ridges, cracks, brine drainage points, attached mats, aggregates, and areas of grazing or sloughing.

### 3.5 Prokaryotes mediate carbon, sulfur, and nitrogen transformations

Recurring ice-associated bacteria include representatives related to *Polaribacter*, *Glaciecola*, *Paraglaciecola*, *Colwellia*, *Octadecabacter*, *Marinobacter*, *Psychromonas*, *Pseudoalteromonas*, and other Flavobacteriales, Alteromonadales, Oceanospirillales, and Rhodobacterales. Their relative abundances vary by season and habitat and should not be treated as a universal “core” list.

Metagenomes indicate potential for:

- Degradation of algal polysaccharides and other complex organic matter.
- DMSP degradation and sulfur oxidation.
- Dissimilatory nitrate reduction and partial or complete denitrification.
- Ammonia oxidation, especially during darker periods.
- Osmolyte transport and metabolism, extracellular polymer processing, cold adaptation, oxidative-stress response, and DNA repair.

These functions have been resolved in community metagenomes and MAGs ([Yergeau et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep42242); [Rapp et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2021.701186); [Bellas et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43705-023-00337-2); [Venkatachalam et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.01291-25)). They show what organisms may be able to do, not necessarily which pathway was active at collection.

### 3.6 Microbial eukaryotes are diverse and functionally important

Sea ice supports pennate and centric diatoms, dinoflagellates, ciliates, cercozoans, haptophytes, chlorophytes, cryptophytes, stramenopiles, amoebae, fungi, and numerous poorly classified lineages. Strict phototrophs dominate some spring bottom-ice communities, whereas mixotrophs, grazers, and parasites can be important during winter, bloom decline, and melt ([Eddie et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1462-2920.2009.02126.x); [Bachy et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2011.00106); [Piwosz et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2013.39)).

Fungi are not merely contaminants. Chytrids can dominate Arctic marine fungal signals and are associated with stressed or infected algae ([Hassett & Gradinger, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/1462-2920.13216)). Arctic ice and water also contain fungal biomass and genes consistent with osmotrophy, degradation, and parasitism ([Hassett et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41396-019-0368-1)). The high-frequency observations of [Dilliplaine et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1462-2920.70211) suggest that chytrids, *Cryothecomonas*, and other parasitoids can rapidly alter bloom composition.

### 3.7 Viruses are diverse, abundant, and poorly catalogued

Arctic ice viromics reveals extensive novelty: in [Zhong et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1128/msystems.00246-20), only a minority of recovered viral populations could be taxonomically assigned. Putative hosts included common cold-adapted heterotrophs, and viral auxiliary genes suggested effects on host physiology. Long-read Arctic water time series likewise reveal strong seasonality and associations with Flavobacteriaceae, Pelagibacteraceae, and Nitrosopumilaceae ([Calayag et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-61568-6)). Viral and bacterial methylation patterns differ across ice horizons and may reflect regulation, restriction–modification systems, prophages, and recent infection ([Kanaan & Deming, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/ismejo/wraf198)).

A 0.22-µm cellular filter captures intracellular viruses, prophages, particle-associated particles, and some large viruses, but it misses much of the free-virus fraction. Surface-microlayer and melt-pond work demonstrates the value of explicit size fractionation and viral concentration ([Rahlff et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40168-024-01902-0)).

### 3.8 Melting connects ice to the ocean and the seafloor

Ice-algal aggregates have been observed and collected below melting ice ([Assmy et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0076599)). Bacterial community tracking indicates that aggregates transport ice-associated organisms and carbon into the water column and toward the seafloor ([Rapp et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01035)). Recent microcosm evidence directly links melting ice to the release of bacteria, microeukaryotes, aggregates, and organic molecules, although only a subset of ice signatures remains detectable in meltwater ([Cardozo-Mino et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsr2.2026.105607)). The underside is therefore both a habitat and an export boundary.

## 4. Specific assessment of diver-assisted collection

### 4.1 What precedent exists?

Diver access to the underside is not unprecedented. [Assmy et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0076599) used scuba divers, an inverted-trap approach, and under-ice video to collect and quantify floating ice-algal aggregates. [Fernández-Méndez et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2018.00075) incorporated scuba-based observations and collections when studying algal hotspots associated with ridges and snow–ice interfaces. [Summers et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00300-026-03462-5) used divers with a suction sampler (“slurp gun”) to collect replicate underside ice-algal biofilm samples for bio-optical and community measurements.

These studies show that divers can target visible underside features and obtain spatially explicit samples. Their primary emphasis was ice algae, aggregates, biomass, pigments, or optics—not an integrated survey of 16S, 18S/ITS, shotgun metagenomes, free-virus metagenomes, absolute abundances, and geochemistry.

**Novelty judgment:** Within the literature reviewed, diver sampling is established, but a standardized, replicated diver-to-filter workflow for a multi-domain, genome-resolved bottom-ice microbiome is not yet a mature or common design. This is a literature-based inference, not proof that no such unpublished or locally reported study exists.

### 4.2 What will a diver-collected filter actually represent?

Interpretation depends on collection method:

| Collection method | Best sample label | Main bias |
|---|---|---|
| Cut or broken piece with known underside thickness | Bottom-ice section | Breakage and melting may lose brine and fragile material. |
| Scrape over a measured area | Undersurface biofilm | Enriches attached biomass; excludes organisms deeper in the skeletal layer. |
| Suction/slurp gun over a measured area and time | Undersurface slurry | Mixes biofilm, loosened ice, aggregates, and under-ice water. |
| Hand-collected floating aggregate | Ice-algal aggregate | A selected hotspot, not representative of average bottom ice. |
| Unmeasured ice pieces combined across a dive | Composite ice material | Cannot yield area-, volume-, or habitat-normalized ecological estimates. |

The phrase “the diver collects some ice, filters it, and saves the membrane” is therefore insufficient for publication-quality inference. At minimum, every filter needs a sample ID linked to position, collection geometry, original solid mass or volume, added liquid, final melt volume, filtered volume, visual habitat, time, depth, ice thickness, snow cover, temperature, salinity, and storage history.

### 4.3 A publishable comparative design

At each station or floe, collect independent replicates of:

1. **Diver underside sample:** a standardized scrape or suction collection from a measured area, with photographs or video and no compositing among habitats.
2. **Top-down core, bottom 3–5 cm:** the conventional bottom-ice comparator.
3. **Core interior and surface horizons:** necessary if conclusions will refer to the entire ice column.
4. **Under-ice seawater:** collected adjacent to, but not after disturbing, the sampling patch.
5. **Visible aggregate or ridge habitat:** collected as a separate category rather than folded into average underside material.
6. **Controls:** field exposure, sterile collection-device rinse, diver-equipment rinse where feasible, melt solution, filtration, extraction, and library blanks.

Use at least three independent biological replicates per habitat per station; five is preferable where underside patchiness is a primary question. Keep stations or floes as higher-level replicates. Do not pool core horizons or diver patches before DNA extraction or shotgun sequencing.

### 4.4 Filtration and molecular analyses

A practical multi-domain design would retain:

- **>3 or >5 µm fraction:** large algae, protists, fungi, aggregates, and particle-attached bacteria.
- **0.22–3/5 µm fraction:** most bacteria, archaea, picoeukaryotes, and small particles.
- **<0.22 µm filtrate:** concentrated separately for free DNA-virus analysis; an independent RNA-virus workflow is required for RNA viruses.
- **Unfiltered aliquots:** microscopy, flow cytometry, chlorophyll, particulate carbon and nitrogen, and dissolved chemistry.

Analyze 16S for bacteria and archaea, 18S for broad eukaryotes, and ITS if fungi are a central objective. Shotgun metagenomics should be performed on each independent sample, not only on DNA pooled across layers. Representative long-read libraries can improve MAGs, strain resolution, plasmids, virus–host links, and methylation analysis. Use quantitative cell counts, qPCR, or internal standards because relative read abundance alone cannot establish absolute change.

### 4.5 Melting and contamination are decisive

Melting method changes the observed community. Unbuffered melting can expose organisms to hypo-osmotic shock, whereas natural seawater can add cells, extracellular DNA, or viruses. [Chamberlain et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1525/elementa.2022.00017) directly showed that melting procedures altered cell counts and bacterial and eukaryotic community measurements. [Campbell et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.3389/feart.2019.00021) independently demonstrated effects of melting procedure on ice-algal physiology.

Before the main campaign, compare a direct melt with a defined isohaline or artificial-seawater-buffered melt. Process the melt solution as a sequenced blank. Melt at a consistent low temperature in darkness, minimize holding time, record the dilution, and express biomass and chemistry relative to the original ice or sampled underside area. Natural seawater should not be used as a buffer unless its microbial and extracellular-DNA contribution is explicitly quantified.

Diver work also introduces distinctive contamination pathways: gloves, suit surfaces, buoyancy-compensator exhaust, breathing bubbles, collection bags, suction tubing, ship water, and previously sampled material. The diver should approach down-current where possible, collect the under-ice-water control before disturbing the surface, use sterile or validated clean tools and bags, and change or decontaminate tools between biological replicates. Sequenced controls are necessary because some interior and winter samples will have low biomass.

| Priority gap | Why it matters | How this project could address it |
|---|---|---|
| Direct, spatially resolved underside microbiomes | Most molecular studies use top-down cores, expressed brine, melt ponds, or under-ice water. The attached underside may be patchier and more active than a homogenized bottom-core section. | Pair measured-area diver scrapes or suction samples with immediately adjacent bottom cores and water controls. |
| Centimeter-to-meter patchiness | Visible brown films, ridges, cracks, and drainage points may create microbial hotspots that are lost by pooling. | Map each patch with calibrated imagery, sample independent patches, and model spatial variance. |
| Representation of complex ice | Ridges and rotten ice can be biomass-rich and compositionally distinct but are poorly represented in routine level-ice coring. | Stratify sampling by level ice, ridge, lead, rotten ice, aggregate, and melt stage. |
| Full annual cycle | Winter, freeze-up, early spring, peak bloom, post-bloom, and late melt are rarely sampled at one location with the same methods. | Repeat the same locations and protocol across seasons or autonomous revisit periods. |
| Interannual and long-term change | Snapshot surveys cannot distinguish climate trends from local variability. | Establish fixed stations, frozen protocols, archived filters, and openly versioned metadata for repeated years. |
| Activity rather than potential | DNA cannot prove that a pathway is operating or quantify its rate. | Pair DNA with RNA, proteins or metabolites, and direct carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur process measurements. |
| Absolute abundance and biomass | Relative sequence changes can arise even when the focal taxon does not change in cell number. | Add microscopy, flow cytometry, chlorophyll, qPCR/dPCR, spike-ins, and area- or volume-normalized biomass. |
| Fungi, parasitoids, and trophic interactions | These groups can regulate algal blooms but are missed by prokaryote-only or 0.22-µm-only studies. | Retain a large-particle fraction, sequence 18S and ITS, and use microscopy or imaging to confirm infections. |
| Free viruses, RNA viruses, and ultrasmall cells | Cellular filters provide only a partial viral view. | Analyze the <0.22-µm fraction, use capsid-protected DNA/RNA workflows, and pair viruses with host MAGs and CRISPR or linkage evidence. |
| Strain variation and mobile elements | Closely related ice bacteria may have different substrate use, stress genes, plasmids, and phages. | Add high-molecular-weight DNA and long reads to selected high-biomass samples. |
| Epigenetic regulation | Methylation has been demonstrated in one vertically resolved study, but its environmental drivers and functional consequences remain uncertain. | Preserve samples rapidly and integrate nanopore methylation with expression and environmental gradients. |
| Viability and relic DNA | Filters contain DNA from living, dormant, damaged, and dead cells plus extracellular DNA. | Compare DNA and RNA, use viability-sensitive approaches cautiously, and measure extracellular DNA where feasible. |
| Causal environmental controls | Salinity, temperature, light, nutrients, ice permeability, and algal carbon covary. | Collect matched physical and chemical data and use experiments or repeated natural gradients to separate drivers. |
| Pollutants and interacting climate stressors | Shipping, extraction, and spill risks are changing while warmer, more permeable ice may alter exposure and recovery. | Archive baseline samples and, if relevant, use controlled oil, dispersant, microplastic, or contaminant experiments separate from observational samples. |
| Method comparability | Melt solution, time, filter material, pore size, extraction kit, and pooling can change results. | Run a pre-study method experiment and publish a frozen standard operating procedure with complete blanks. |
| Reference-database incompleteness | Many Arctic eukaryotic and viral reads remain unclassified, and MAGs do not cover all habitats. | Deposit reads, assemblies, MAGs, viral genomes, images, and curated metadata; retain voucher DNA and filters when possible. |

## 6. Recommended scientific framing

A defensible central objective is:

> Determine how the taxonomic composition, functional potential, absolute abundance, and cross-domain interactions of Arctic bottom-ice microbiomes vary across undersurface microhabitats, and test whether diver-collected undersurface samples capture ecological information that is missed or diluted by conventional top-down ice cores.

Strong research questions include:

1. Are diver-collected undersurface biofilms compositionally and functionally different from the adjacent bottom 3–5 cm of a conventional core?
2. How much variation occurs among patches separated by centimeters, meters, stations, and ice types?
3. Which bacterial, archaeal, eukaryotic, fungal, and viral populations are consistently associated with algae-rich, ridge, crack, or rotten-ice habitats?
4. Do genes for algal-polysaccharide degradation, DMSP transformation, sulfur oxidation, ammonia oxidation, nitrate reduction, and stress adaptation vary with measured nutrients, salinity, light, and biomass?
5. Which of these genes are expressed, and which corresponding processes can be measured directly?
6. Does the <0.22-µm virome identify virus–host relationships and functions not recovered from cellular filters?
7. During melt, which organisms and molecules remain attached, which enter under-ice water, and which form sinking aggregates?

### Suggested claims to avoid

- “This is the first study of microorganisms in Arctic sea ice.”
- “A 0.22-µm filter represents the complete microbiome.”
- “Metagenomic genes prove ecological activity.”
- “One bottom-ice sample represents the entire ice column.”
- “Relative abundance is equivalent to cell abundance.”
- “All reads on a low-biomass filter are indigenous to the ice.”

### A stronger novelty statement

> Previous studies have characterized Arctic sea-ice microbial diversity, functional potential, viruses, and vertical and seasonal variation, while diver-based collections have primarily targeted ice algae and aggregates. This study will use standardized diver-assisted sampling to resolve the spatially heterogeneous bottom-ice–water interface and directly compare it with conventional cores, under-ice water, and free-virus fractions.

## 7. Overall conclusion

The proposed study is scientifically justified, but it should be positioned as an advance in **sampling resolution, habitat coverage, cross-domain integration, and ecological validation**, not as the first Arctic sea-ice microbiome survey. Existing work already shows that sea ice contains active and highly structured microbial ecosystems with important roles in primary production, organic-matter degradation, sulfur and nitrogen cycling, viral mortality, parasitism, and export during melt.

The clearest opportunity is to exploit what a diver can do better than a conventional core: observe the underside, target defined microhabitats, preserve spatial context, and collect attached material before it is diluted into under-ice water. The decisive comparison is not simply “ice versus water,” but **diver-collected undersurface biofilm versus adjacent bottom-core ice versus under-ice water**, repeated across ice structures and seasons. With appropriate fractionation, controls, absolute measurements, environmental metadata, and activity assays, this could make a genuinely new and durable contribution.
